## Core Rules & Mental Models

**The master rule.** Break any rule below only when you can name the compensating merit you buy with the violation. Strunk: "The best writers sometimes disregard the rules of rhetoric. When they do so, however, the reader will usually find in the sentence some compensating merit, attained at the cost of the violation. Unless he is certain of doing as well, he will probably do best to follow the rules."

**Rule 17 — Omit needless words.** The book's centre. "A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts... but that **every word tell**." Not "write short" — carry no passengers. *the fact that* is to be "revised out of every sentence in which it occurs."

**Rules 14, 15, 16 — one lever, three ways.** Active voice over passive; positive form over *not*; the concrete over the abstract. Each shortens the sentence as a side effect: "when a sentence is made stronger, it usually becomes shorter." Brevity is a by-product of vigor, not a target. *A period of unfavorable weather set in* → *It rained every day for a week.*

**Rule 19 — Parallel construction.** Likeness of form lets the reader see likeness of content. Mixed forms read as timidity — "unable or afraid to choose one form of expression and hold to it."

**Rules 20, 22 — Position carries meaning.** Keep related words together; put the *new* element (the logical predicate) at the end. The beginning is the other emphatic position, but only for non-subjects. Scales from words in a sentence to paragraphs in a composition.

**Rule 3 / *that* vs. *which* — Restrictive or not?** Does the clause tell the reader *which one*? If yes → restrictive → *that*, no commas. If it merely adds a fact → nonrestrictive → *which*, commas both sides. Never one comma without its mate. Rules 3–6 "should be so thoroughly mastered that their application becomes second nature."

**Rule 12 / Reminder 3 — Work from a suitable design.** "You raise a pup tent from one sort of vision, a cathedral from another."

**Reminder 4 — Write with nouns and verbs.** "The adjective hasn't been built that can pull a weak or inaccurate noun out of a tight place."

**Reminder 8 — Avoid qualifiers.** "*Rather, very, little, pretty* — these are the leeches that infest the pond of prose, sucking the blood of words."

**Reminder 1 — Place yourself in the background.** "To achieve style, begin by affecting none." Style is nondetachable — not a sauce added to dull prose but an expression of self. "A careful and honest writer does not need to worry about style."

**Reminder 16 — Be clear.** "Clarity, clarity, clarity." When mired in a sentence, start fresh rather than fight the syntax; usually it needs breaking into two.

**Reminders 5, 14, 21 — Revise; trust the ear; err conservative.** "It is no sign of weakness or defeat that your manuscript ends up in need of major surgery." On split infinitives and terminal prepositions "the ear must be quicker than the handbook" — but an ear comes from reading well, so where it is untrained, prefer the standard to the offbeat.

## Scope & Limits

Synthesized summaries of the 4th edition, not the book's text. It covers plain English style — "it does not pretend to survey the whole field." Its usage judgments date from 1979–2000 and some have since shifted, notably singular *they*, which ch05 records the book rejecting. For house style, defer to your style guide; for contested usage, check a current dictionary.

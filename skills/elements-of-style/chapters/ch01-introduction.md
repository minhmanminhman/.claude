# Chapter 1: Introduction — Strunk's Stance (E. B. White, 1979)

## Core Idea
The book's authority comes from a posture, not a rulebook: cleanliness, accuracy, and brevity, delivered as direct orders, out of sympathy for a reader who is "in serious trouble most of the time."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The little book's scope**: seven rules of usage, eleven principles of composition, a few matters of form, a list of misused words — a triage list for the violations that actually occur, not a grammar reference. Fix what is "most commonly violated" first.
- **Rules as commands**: every rule is an imperative ("Do not join independent clauses with a comma"), followed by a short hortatory essay and parallel columns — true vs. false, bold vs. timid.
- **The compensating-merit test** (Strunk's own escape hatch): "The best writers sometimes disregard the rules of rhetoric. When they do so, however, the reader will usually find in the sentence some compensating merit, attained at the cost of the violation. Unless he is certain of doing as well, he will probably do best to follow the rules." Any time you want to break a rule, name the merit you are buying. If you cannot name it, follow the rule.

## Key Concepts
- **Parvum opus** — Strunk's attempt to write the rules of English rhetoric "on the head of a pin."
- **Drain the swamp** — the writer's duty to the floundering reader: get them onto dry ground, or at least throw a rope.
- **Boldness over correctness** — Strunk "felt it was worse to be irresolute than to be wrong." His original Rule 11 was "Make definite assertions."

## Mental Models
- Think of yourself as **Sergeant Strunk's platoon**: the rules are orders. Obedience is the default; deviation is a decision you must justify.
- Every sentence owes the reader **a debt of clarity** — muddiness is not a stylistic flaw but a failure of duty.
- Brevity is **a by-product of vigor**, not a target: "when a sentence is made stronger, it usually becomes shorter."

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the rules as matters of taste.** They partly are — but that is not licence to skip them. Strunk acknowledged "the fallacy of inflexibility and the danger of doctrine" while still insisting you follow the rules unless certain of doing better.
- **Hiding behind hedges.** "If you don't know how to pronounce a word, say it loud!" Inaudibility compounds ignorance.

# Chapter 2: I. Elementary Rules of Usage (Rules 1–11)

## Core Idea
Eleven mechanical rules, mostly punctuation, that resolve the ambiguities readers actually trip over. Rules 3–6 are load-bearing: "They should be so thoroughly mastered that their application becomes second nature."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rule 1 — Form the possessive singular by adding `'s`**, whatever the final consonant: *Charles's friend*, *Burns's poems*, *the witch's malice*.
  - Exceptions: ancient proper names in *-es*/*-is*, *Jesus'*, *for conscience' sake*. Prefer *the laws of Moses* to *Moses' Laws*.
  - Pronominal possessives take no apostrophe (*hers, its, theirs, yours, ours*); indefinite pronouns do (*one's rights*, *somebody else's umbrella*).
- **Rule 2 — Serial comma.** In a series of three or more terms with one conjunction, comma after each term except the last: *red, white, and blue*. Firm names follow the firm's own usage.
- **Rule 3 — Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas.** The rule with the widest reach.
  - Test: does the clause *identify* the antecedent (restrictive → no commas) or merely *add* to it (nonrestrictive → commas)?
  - *People who live in glass houses shouldn't throw stones* (restrictive). *The audience, which had at first been indifferent, became interested* (nonrestrictive).
  - Never omit one comma and leave the other.
  - Also parenthetic: dates (*April 6, 1986*), names in direct address, *etc./i.e./e.g.*, degrees, trailing titles. Not parenthetic: a restrictive term of identification (*Billy the Kid*, *the novelist Jane Austen*).
- **Rule 4 — Comma before a conjunction introducing an independent clause.** Same for *as* ("because"), *for*, *or*, *nor*, *while* ("at the same time"). When the subject is stated once: keep the comma before *but*, drop it before *and* if the relation is close.
- **Rule 5 — Do not join independent clauses with a comma.** Use a semicolon; a period is equally correct but weaker. With a conjunction, use a comma (Rule 4).
  - Semicolon still required before *accordingly, besides, then, therefore, thus*.
  - Exception: very short, parallel, conversational clauses take commas — *Man proposes, God disposes.*
- **Rule 6 — Do not break sentences in two.** Do not use periods for commas. A fragment is permissible only as deliberate emphasis (*Again and again he called out. No reply.*) or in clipped dialogue.
- **Rule 7 — Colon after an independent clause** to introduce a list, appositive, amplification, or quotation. Never separate a verb from its complement or a preposition from its object with one.
- **Rule 8 — Dash for an abrupt break or a long appositive.** Stronger than a comma, less formal than a colon, more relaxed than parentheses. Use only when a commoner mark is inadequate; never two dashed interruptions in one sentence.
- **Rule 9 — Number of the subject determines the number of the verb.** Intervening words do not change it.
  - *one of those who **have***; singular after *each, either, everyone, everybody, neither, nobody, someone*.
  - *none* = "no one/not one" → singular; *none* suggesting several → plural.
  - Subject stays singular through *with, as well as, in addition to, except, together with, no less than*.
- **Rule 10 — Use the proper case of pronoun.** *who/whom* takes its case from its function in its own clause: *the candidate **who** we think will win* (we think **he** will win) vs. *the candidate **whom** we hope to elect* (we hope to elect **him**).
  - Supply "understood" verbs: *Polly loves cake more than she loves me.*
  - Gerunds take the possessive (*Mother objected to **our** driving*); participles take the objective (*They heard **him** singing*).
  - Never *myself/yourself* as a subject: *Blake and **I** stayed home.*
- **Rule 11 — An opening participial phrase must refer to the grammatical subject.** Same for opening appositives, adjectives, and adjective phrases. *Being in a dilapidated condition, I was able to buy the house very cheap* → recast.

## Anti-patterns
- **Half-parenthesis** — one comma without its mate. "There is no defense for such punctuation."
- **Comma splice** where the relation is cause and consequence; the semicolon is "one of the most useful devices of composition."
- **Dangling modifier** — Rule 11 violations "are often ludicrous."

# Chapter 3: II. Elementary Principles of Composition (Rules 12–22)

## Core Idea
The book's centre of gravity. Rules 14–17 (active voice, positive form, concrete language, omit needless words) do most of the work in ordinary prose.

## Rules
- **12 — Choose a suitable design and hold to it.** Foresee the shape and pursue it. "The more clearly the writer perceives the shape, the better are the chances of success." Exception: a love letter (an outpouring) or a casual essay (a ramble) — sometimes the best design is no design.
- **13 — Make the paragraph the unit of composition.** One topic per paragraph; the break signals a new step. Begin with a topic sentence or a transition. Single-sentence paragraphs only for transitions; in dialogue, one per speaker. Split enormous blocks even when sense does not require it — but a run of short paragraphs "read like the writing of commerce or of display advertising."
- **14 — Use the active voice.** Not a ban on the passive, which is often convenient: the need to make a particular word the *subject* decides the voice. Replace perfunctory *there is* / *could be heard*:
  - *There were a great number of dead leaves lying on the ground* → **Dead leaves covered the ground.**
  - *The reason he left college was that his health became impaired* → **Failing health compelled him to leave college.**
- **15 — Put statements in positive form.** Use *not* for denial or antithesis, never evasion. *not honest* → **dishonest**; *did not remember* → **forgot**; *did not pay any attention to* → **ignored**. Antithesis is the strong use: *Not charity, but simple justice.* Save *would, should, could, may, might* for real uncertainty — "if your every sentence admits a doubt, your writing will lack authority."
- **16 — Use definite, specific, concrete language.** "The surest way to arouse and hold the reader's attention is by being specific, definite, and concrete."
  - *A period of unfavorable weather set in* → **It rained every day for a week.**
  - *He showed satisfaction as he took possession of his well-earned reward* → **He grinned as he pocketed the coin.**
- **17 — Omit needless words.** "Vigorous writing is concise... This requires not that the writer make all sentences short, or avoid all detail and treat subjects only in outline, but that **every word tell**."
  - *the question as to whether* → **whether** · *he is a man who* → **he** · *in a hasty manner* → **hastily** · *the reason why is that* → **because**
  - *the fact that* "should be revised out of every sentence in which it occurs": *owing to the fact that* → **since**; *the fact that he had not succeeded* → **his failure**.
  - *who is*, *which was* are usually superfluous: *His cousin, who is a member of the same firm* → *His cousin, a member of the same firm*.
- **18 — Avoid a succession of loose sentences** (two clauses, the second on a conjunction or relative). An occasional one relieves formality; a paragraph of them produces "mechanical symmetry and singsong."
- **19 — Express coordinate ideas in similar form.** Repeat the article or preposition before every term or only before the first: *the French, the Italians, the Spanish, and the Portuguese*. Correlatives (*both/and, not only/but also, either/or*) must take the same construction on each side: *It was both a long ceremony and very tedious* → **The ceremony was both long and tedious.** Twenty parallel ideas? Group them and apply the principle within each group, or use a table.
- **20 — Keep related words together.** Position is the principal means of showing relationship.
  - *He noticed a large stain in the rug that was right in the center* → **He noticed a large stain right in the center of the rug.**
  - Do not separate subject from principal verb by a phrase that could open the sentence: *Toni Morrison, in Beloved, writes...* → **In Beloved, Toni Morrison writes...**
  - Relative pronoun immediately after its antecedent; modifiers next to what they modify: *She only found two mistakes* → **She found only two mistakes.**
- **21 — In summaries, keep to one tense.** Present for drama; present (or past) for poem, story, novel. Antecedent action → perfect; indirect discourse keeps its own past. Signal once that what follows is summary, then stop repeating *he said*. In criticism, aim at "an orderly discussion supported by evidence, not a summary with occasional comment."
- **22 — Place the emphatic words at the end.** The position of prominence belongs to the logical predicate — the new element.
  - *This steel is principally used for making razors, because of its hardness* → **Because of its hardness, this steel is used principally for making razors.**
  - The beginning is the other emphatic position: *Deceit or treachery she could never forgive.* But a *subject* placed first is not emphatic by position alone — to emphasize it, move it to the predicate's slot: *Through the middle of the valley flowed a winding stream.*
  - The principle scales: words in a sentence, sentences in a paragraph, paragraphs in a composition.

## Worked Example
Strunk's own demonstration of Rule 17 — the same content, twice:

> Macbeth was very ambitious. This led him to wish to become king of Scotland. The witches told him that this wish of his would come true. The king of Scotland at this time was Duncan. Encouraged by his wife, Macbeth murdered Duncan. He was thus enabled to succeed Duncan as king. **(51 words)**

> Encouraged by his wife, Macbeth achieved his ambition and realized the prediction of the witches by murdering Duncan and becoming king of Scotland in his place. **(26 words)**

The fault is not long words; it is "a single complex idea, step by step, in a series of sentences that might to advantage be combined into one." Half the words, all the meaning.

## Key Takeaways
1. Rules 14, 15, 17 are one lever pulled three ways: active, positive, and concise all shorten and strengthen. Brevity is the by-product, never the target.
2. Every word must tell. Not "write short" — carry no passengers.
3. Parallel content demands parallel form; the reader reads structure before meaning.
4. Put the new information last. Emphasis is positional, and it scales from clause to composition.
5. Two silent faults to hunt: *the fact that*, and telling the reader what is *not*.

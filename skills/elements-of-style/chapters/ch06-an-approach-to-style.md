# Chapter 6: V. An Approach to Style (21 Reminders) — E. B. White

## Core Idea
"Here we leave solid ground." Chapters I–IV covered what is correct; this covers what is distinguished. Style is not detachable ornament — it is "an expression of self," and the approach to it "is by way of plainness, simplicity, orderliness, sincerity."

**Style is nondetachable.** "Young writers often suppose that style is a garnish for the meat of prose, a sauce by which a dull dish is made palatable. Style has no such separate entity; it is nondetachable, unfilterable." So turn "resolutely away from all devices that are popularly believed to indicate style — all mannerisms, tricks, adornments."

## The 21 Reminders
1. **Place yourself in the background.** "To achieve style, begin by affecting none."
2. **Write in a way that comes naturally.** Language begins in imitation; never imitate consciously, but "take pains instead to admire what is good."
3. **Work from a suitable design.** "You raise a pup tent from one sort of vision, a cathedral from another." Even impetuous writing has a secret plan — "Columbus didn't just sail, he sailed west."
4. **Write with nouns and verbs.** "The adjective hasn't been built that can pull a weak or inaccurate noun out of a tight place."
5. **Revise and rewrite.** "It is no sign of weakness or defeat that your manuscript ends up in need of major surgery."
6. **Do not overwrite.** Guard especially against the seduction of easy typing.
7. **Do not overstate.** "A single overstatement, wherever or however it occurs, diminishes the whole."
8. **Avoid the use of qualifiers.** "*Rather, very, little, pretty* — these are the leeches that infest the pond of prose, sucking the blood of words."
9. **Do not affect a breezy manner.** The breezy style is "the work of an egocentric" who has nothing to say.
10. **Use orthodox spelling.** Unaccepted spellings "distract his attention and exhaust his patience."
11. **Do not explain too much.** Be sparing with adverbs after *he said*; let the dialogue disclose the manner.
12. **Do not construct awkward adverbs.** *tangledly*, *tiredly*, *thusly* — "Words that are not used orally are seldom the ones to put on paper."
13. **Make sure the reader knows who is speaking.** Place attributives where a speaker would pause; test by reading aloud.
14. **Avoid fancy words.** "Do not be tempted by a twenty-dollar word when there is a ten-center handy." Anglo-Saxon over Latin — but *gut* and *intestine* are not interchangeable; context governs. "A matter of ear."
15. **Do not use dialect unless your ear is good.** Be consistent; the best dialect writers "use the minimum, not the maximum, of deviation from the norm."
16. **Be clear.** "Clarity, clarity, clarity." When mired, start fresh rather than fight the syntax — usually the sentence needs breaking into two.
17. **Do not inject opinion.** "Opinions scattered indiscriminately about leave the mark of egotism on a work."
18. **Use figures of speech sparingly.** Never mix a metaphor — "don't start by calling something a swordfish and end by calling it an hourglass."
19. **Do not take shortcuts at the cost of clarity.** Write names out in full first, abbreviate later.
20. **Avoid foreign languages.** "Write in English."
21. **Prefer the standard to the offbeat.** Between formal and informal, orthodox and heretical, "the beginner err on the side of conservatism."

## Mental Models
- **The writer as gunner**: "sometimes waiting in the blind for something to come in, sometimes roaming the countryside hoping to scare something up... working many covers to bring down one partridge." Writing is slow because the mind outruns the pen.
- **The ear as final authority**: on split infinitives, terminal prepositions, *that*-omission, *I* vs. *me* — "the ear must be quicker than the handbook." *"A claw hammer, not an ax, was the tool he murdered her with"* beats the grammatical version "because it sounds more violent, more like murder."
- **Audience of one**: sympathize with the reader's plight "but never seek to know the reader's wants. Your whole duty as a writer is to please and satisfy yourself."

## Worked Example
Style is not paraphrasable. Paine's *"These are the times that try men's souls"* — eight ordinary words, three centuries of durability. The variants:

> Times like these try men's souls. / How trying it is to live in these times! / These are trying times for men's souls. / Soulwise, these are trying times.

"No fault of grammar can be detected in them, and in every case the meaning is clear. Each version is correct, and each, for some reason that we can't readily put our finger on, is marked for oblivion." White refuses to explain why. The usable lesson: correctness is a floor, not a ceiling; the rest is ear.

Reminder 8 demonstrated on itself: *"we should all try to do a **little** better, we should all be **very** watchful of this rule, for it is a **rather** important one, and we are **pretty** sure to violate it now and then."*

## Key Takeaways
1. Style emerges from competence, not pursuit. "A careful and honest writer does not need to worry about style."
2. Nouns and verbs carry meaning; adjectives cannot rescue a weak one.
3. Revision is not remedial — it is the work.
4. Delete *rather, very, little, pretty* on sight.
5. When a sentence is hopeless, start over; do not fight the syntax.
6. The ear overrules the handbook — but only an ear trained by reading well. Untrained, err conservative.
7. "Writing is an act of faith, not a trick of grammar."
